## Why it's hard to answer

For those of you who aren't in the tech world or in the engineering
space: it was the inflection point where AI got so good at coding that
developers stopped typing code (and even correcting manually) and moved
over to accepting and inspecting diffs (if some even do that). If the
code had a bug the natural next step was to paste the bug to your coding
agent and expect it to magic fix everything. And it almost always does —
don't get me wrong, it's incredible — but somewhere along the way this
started to get more and more good, and I started to off-load more and
more of my thinking work to it.

Designing a table schema — take it, whatever you think is right, and
efficient. I was pinning so much of my hope on the word *efficient* to
get the right schema.

This blog post is an explanation of how quickly things have changed and
a personal note on my plight in this AI-driven everything: you have a
problem, let me build an app for it — and most of those apps are bad.
There are so many shitty applications on the internet right now, and I
honestly don't know why, like many competitive exams, we aren't giving
negative marks for shitty codebases.

## The list

A few things stuck. Most came from people, not prompts.

**From co-workers:**
* **StepFunctions** for testing parts of a pipeline and not re-running
  the whole thing.
* **Make pre-commit** to check your ruff/lint/mypy checks before letting
  the GitHub Actions do its thing. (GitHub Actions can quickly compound
  in cost over time and this has been a good alternative for me.)

**On my own:**
* I learnt about **uv** by watching a full 27-minute YouTube video and
  not asking the AI about it.
* **Deploying a Lambda function on AWS** — though I'd have to refresh
  my memory.

## What I noticed

Something that bugs me — the things AI teaches me don't stick most of
the time. Not as much as hearing about it in a conversation with human
beings, or reading someone's blog to get a richer understanding of
things.

There have been good times. Skill exploration has helped me stay on top
of the advancements, and hearing how people use AI has been fascinating
and daunting at times. I do follow a few people on the internet who
constantly teach me new things and introduce me to new tools that make
my workflow better — both in my understanding and in the speed at which
I'm able to deliver things.

## An aside on the algorithm

I do have a love-hate relationship with Twitter right now. The
algorithm has been good at surfacing relevant topics to me, but most of
the people posting under those topics seem to be using the same AI
prompt — which you can see coming from a mile away: excessive em-dashes,
"[X] killed [Y]" framings, every point posed as a question-and-answer,
too much use of the phrase *"the catch is?"*. All of these are going
on my block list on X.

## The landing

There should be things I learn on a weekly basis — that's my point. I
should not struggle to think about what I learnt. This should be
ingrained and be part of my routine.
